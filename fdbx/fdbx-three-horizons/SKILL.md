# Three Horizons

## Overview

Three Horizons is a practice for thinking and talking about transformation, developed by Bill Sharpe and Anthony Hodgson with the International Futures Forum. It draws three patterns of how things are done in an area of concern, each as a line over time. The height of each line shows how prevalent the pattern is:

- **First horizon (H1)** — business as usual. The established pattern that daily life depends on. Most change inside it is incremental and reinforces it. The conversation starts from the recognition that H1 is **losing its fit** with emerging conditions.
- **Third horizon (H3)** — the emerging pattern that will eventually succeed H1. It already exists at the margins, as **pockets of the future in the present**. There are usually several contested H3 views, not one.
- **Second horizon (H2)** — the turbulent space of transition, where people try out innovations in response to H1's failings and H3's possibilities. It is a space of both conflicts and options, marked by clashes of values. Some innovations (**H2+**) help H3 emerge. Others (**H2−**) are absorbed back into H1 and only prop it up.

All three patterns are **present at the same time**, at different strengths. Change always starts in the present.

> **Not the McKinsey model.** The first published "Three Horizons" (Baghai, Coley & White, *The Alchemy of Growth*, 1999) is a management model of short-, medium- and long-term growth, drawn as successive curves, each more valuable than the last. The futures practice this skill uses changed that fundamentally: the horizons exist *in parallel* and describe patterns of a system, not a portfolio of growth bets (Curry & Hodgson, 2008). Do not produce "core / adjacent / transformational" business lines.

The practice is deliberately simple: the framework can be explained in minutes. Its authors present it as propositions from about 180 uses, not as a tested method. They also say the facilitator's expertise strongly affects how deep a session goes.

> **fdbx adaptation:** we point the map at a product, service or organisation and add a **portfolio classification**. Every current initiative is placed as H1 maintenance, H2+, H2−, or H3 pocket, with a reason. The source describes H2+/− as something to explore with participants; we make the exploration explicit for each initiative.

## Use This Skill When

- The current model is visibly losing fit, and the team needs to plan the transition, not just the destination.
- A team has lots of "innovation" and no idea which of it actually leads anywhere new.
- People responsible for running today's system and people pushing for change are talking past each other.
- You have a vision (for example from `fdbx-four-futures`) and need a pathway from here.
- You need a shared, quick framework at the start of a longer futures or strategy project.

## Inputs

- The area of concern: product, service, organisation, sector, or system.
- A horizon length. This varies by domain: long for infrastructure (Curry & Hodgson's energy example puts H3 about 50 years out), shorter for fast-moving digital services.
- Current initiatives, projects and experiments, if the user wants the portfolio classification.
- Who is involved: those who run today's system, those experimenting, those holding a vision.
- Evidence of stress in H1 and of pockets of H3. These go into the Evidence Ledger with their evidence type (`user-supplied`, `sourced`, or `assumption`).

## Workflow

The five steps follow Sharpe et al.'s description of a typical session. Steps 6 and 7 are the "additional steps" they describe for moving from sense-making to action.

### Step 1 — Present concerns (H1)

Describe how things are done now and the evidence that this is **losing its fit**: rising cost, falling relevance, new conditions it cannot meet. Name who maintains H1 and how. Note where incremental improvements make the underlying problem worse — the "fixes that fail" pattern.

### Step 2 — Future aspirations (H3)

Describe the visions and possibilities that could replace H1 over the horizon. Expect more than one. Note where they conflict.

### Step 3 — Pockets of the future in the present

Find **concrete existing examples** of H3 already happening, at the margins or elsewhere in the world. Each one gets an Evidence Ledger row. A pocket the model supplies from training is an `assumption` until the user confirms or sources it.

### Step 4 — Innovations in play (H2)

List the innovations and experiments happening in the transition space. For each, judge whether it is **H2+** (opens space for H3) or **H2−** (captured by H1; improves the old system on its own terms), and say why. The judgement is open to discussion, so state the reasoning.

### Step 5 — Essential features to maintain

Name what in H1 is worth carrying into the future: capabilities, relationships, values, infrastructure. The practice resists treating H1 as simply bad and H3 as simply good.

### Step 6 — Dilemmas and power

- **Dilemma:** frame the central tension between an H1 value and an H3 value as two things that must both be respected, e.g. "optimise the current service" and "invest in the next one". Dilemmas are not solved once; they are re-solved through experiments.
- **Power:** who can resist or bring about the change, and what each actor can realistically do.

### Step 7 — Three horizon perspectives and actions

Write how each horizon sees the others, first as a **fixed mindset** (negative) and then as a **perspective** (positive). The H1 manager sees H3 as fanciful; the H3 visionary sees H1 as a dinosaur. Once each side sees the others' value, the conversation opens up. See `references/horizon-perspectives.md`.

Then name **actions**: H2+ moves to start or strengthen, H1 features to protect, and pockets of H3 to learn from or connect with.

> **fdbx adaptation — portfolio classification:** if the user listed current initiatives, classify each as H1 maintenance, H2+, H2−, or H3 pocket, with a one-line reason. Then note the balance: are most resources going to H2−?

## Guardrails

- Do not produce McKinsey's growth horizons. No "core / adjacent / transformational" business lines and no successive curves: all three patterns exist now, at different strengths.
- Do not turn the horizons into phases ("H1: next 12 months, H2: 1–3 years, H3: 3–5 years"). A timeline of actions is fine; the horizons themselves are patterns present today.
- Do not treat H1 as bad and H3 as good. Name what must be kept from H1, and say what could go wrong with each H3.
- Pockets of the future must be concrete, existing examples with an Evidence Ledger row, not predictions dressed as examples. Never invent a named initiative or its figures. If you cannot name a real example, say so and describe it as something to look for.
- Every H2 innovation gets a + or − with a reason. "Innovative" is not the same as H2+: many innovations make H1 better at being H1.
- Offer more than one H3 unless the user has already chosen one.
- Set the horizon length by domain and say why.
- Say what the practice is: a framework for dialogue, which its authors present as practice propositions, not a validated predictor.
- Write the section titles as markdown headings (`##`), as in the Output Format. Plain-text titles hide the Evidence Ledger and the Handoff from readers who scan.

## Deliverable Quality Bar

A strong Three Horizons output:

- describes H1 with specific evidence that it is losing fit
- offers at least two H3 aspirations, and at least two concrete pockets of the future in the present, each in the Evidence Ledger
- classifies every listed H2 innovation as H2+ or H2−, with a reason for each
- names at least one H1 feature worth maintaining
- frames a dilemma as two values to be held together, and names who has power to resist or enable change
- writes each horizon's view of the others as both a fixed mindset and a positive perspective
- ends with actions tied to specific horizons
- contains no McKinsey-style core/adjacent/transformational framing
- ends with a Handoff naming the next method and why
- opens with a direct answer to the decision or question the user asked, which the rest of the output supports
