# Causal Layered Analysis

## Overview

Causal Layered Analysis (CLA) is a futures research method developed by Sohail Inayatullah. Its premise is simple and uncomfortable: the way a problem is framed determines the solutions that seem possible and the people expected to deliver them. Change the frame and both change.

CLA reads an issue at four depths:

1. **Litany** — the official, public version: headlines, statistics, the problem as everyone already describes it.
2. **Social causes** — the economic, political, cultural and historical factors that explain the litany. Most expert analysis lives here.
3. **Discourse / worldview** — the deeper structures and ways of seeing that make the issue *this* issue at all. Different worldviews do not just explain the problem differently; they constitute different problems.
4. **Myth / metaphor** — the deep story or archetype underneath, expressed as an image rather than an argument.

CLA does not forecast. It widens the range of futures a team can imagine by showing that the present is one framing among several. It is most useful **before** scenario work, because it gives scenarios different depths rather than different intensities of the same assumption.

> **fdbx adaptation:** we point CLA at the design brief. The brief is usually written at the litany layer, with a solution and a success metric already embedded. Reading the brief as the litany exposes what it takes for granted at every layer below, and moving back up from an alternative metaphor produces a reframed brief the team can compare with the original.

## Use This Skill When

- A brief arrives with the solution already in it ("design an app that…", "add a feature to…").
- The team keeps generating variations of the same idea.
- Stakeholders disagree and seem to be talking about different problems.
- You are about to build scenarios and want them to differ in depth, not only in degree.
- A problem has been "solved" repeatedly and keeps coming back.

## Inputs

- The brief, problem statement, or issue, in the words the organisation uses.
- Who wrote it and who is expected to act on it.
- Context: users, geography, sector, and any cultures or communities whose view of the issue differs from the organisation's.
- Evidence already in hand (research, data) — these go into the Evidence Ledger with their evidence type (`user-supplied`, `sourced`, or `assumption`).

If the cultural context is unknown, ask. CLA is sensitive to whose worldview is treated as the default; Inayatullah's cases repeatedly show the deeper layers shifting once other civilisational or community perspectives are included.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — State the litany

Write the issue as it is officially described. For each layer, the output records four things, following the column structure in Inayatullah's summary table:

- **Problem** — how the issue appears at this layer
- **Solution** — the response this framing makes obvious
- **Problem-solver** — who is expected to act
- **Where it lives** — where this framing is written down (the news; op-eds and policy journals; fringe or peripheral journals; the work of artists)

At the litany layer, solutions tend to be short-term, and responsibility is placed with someone else — government, management, "the platform". The emotional register is helplessness, apathy, or "someone should do something".

> **fdbx adaptation:** quote the brief verbatim as the litany, including its success metric. The metric is often the clearest expression of the litany's worldview.

### Step 2 — Social causes

Explain the litany with economic, political, cultural, technological and historical factors. Solutions at this layer are typically systemic fixes: better coordination, efficiency, partnerships between institutions. Note which data or research supports each cause, and put it in the Evidence Ledger.

### Step 3 — Discourse / worldview

Ask what way of seeing makes the issue look like this. Look for structures that stay the same whoever the actors are. Name **at least two discourses** that frame the issue differently — for example economic vs. communal, clinical vs. relational, individual vs. collective — and show how each constitutes a different problem. Each discourse can seed its own scenario; that spread is CLA's horizontal dimension.

Probe with the five questions from Inayatullah's poststructural toolbox (see `references/layer-guide.md`): who is privileged and who is silenced; which discourses won and how; what would make the present look strange; which past is taken as true; how knowledge is ordered and who is othered.

Change at this layer is slower and harder. The actors are typically people and voluntary associations rather than institutions.

### Step 4 — Myth / metaphor

Name the deep story underneath: an archetype, a proverb, a cultural image. Write it as an image or a short line, not an abstract noun — "the doctor knows best", "bigger is better", "the university as a prison". This layer is felt rather than argued; write it so a reader feels it. Here the actors are leaders and artists, and change comes through new stories.

In the Layer Table, fill the myth row like the others. Its **Solution** is the new story or image, often the alternative metaphor below. Its **Problem-solver** is whoever can tell that story: leaders, artists, the community itself.

Then propose **an alternative metaphor** that would make a different future possible.

### Step 5 — Move back up

CLA is not complete at the bottom. Starting from the alternative metaphor, rebuild each layer upward: what worldview follows from it, what systemic changes that worldview would pursue, and what the litany — the public description of the problem — would become. The movement down and back up is what turns better categories into better action.

> **fdbx adaptation:** the rebuilt litany is written as a **reframed brief**, in the same format as the original, so the team can put the two side by side.

### Step 6 — Scenarios by layer

Write one short scenario per layer. They must differ in kind: a litany scenario is instrumental (more of the obvious fix); a social-cause scenario is about policy and systems; a worldview scenario reflects a fundamentally different way of seeing; a myth scenario is told as a story, image, or poem.

### Step 7 — Design implications

State what the analysis changes for the design work: which parts of the original brief still hold, which should be questioned with the client, and what research would test the deeper-layer claims.

## Guardrails

- Do not stop at social causes. That is where conventional analysis stays; CLA's value is in the two layers below.
- Do not argue over which layer an idea belongs in. The layers overlap by design; they are there to open thinking, not to be filed correctly. If something fits nowhere, make a new category.
- Do not write the myth layer as an abstract noun ("capitalism", "individualism"). Write the story people carry.
- Do not leave any cell of the Layer Table empty, the myth row included. A myth has a solution (a new story) and a problem-solver (who tells it).
- Do not end at the bottom. Without moving back up, CLA produces insight and no action. Inayatullah names paralysis as the method's main risk.
- Do not present CLA as a forecast. It maps framings, not probabilities.
- Do not treat the organisation's worldview as neutral. Name it as one discourse among the others.
- Put every statistic, trend, or research finding in the Evidence Ledger with its type. A cause stated from model knowledge is an `assumption`.

## Deliverable Quality Bar

A strong CLA output:

- fills all four layers, each with problem, solution, problem-solver, and where it lives
- names a different problem-solver at different layers rather than the same actor throughout
- identifies at least two distinct discourses at the worldview layer, each constituting a different problem
- states the root metaphor as an image or short line, and proposes an alternative metaphor
- moves back up from the alternative metaphor to a reframed brief in the same format as the original
- gives one scenario per layer, and the four differ in kind rather than intensity
- records every factual or trend claim in the Evidence Ledger with its type
- ends with a Handoff naming the next method and why
- opens with a direct answer to the decision or question the user asked, which the rest of the output supports
