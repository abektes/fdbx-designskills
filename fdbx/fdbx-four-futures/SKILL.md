# Four Futures

## Overview

Jim Dator, of the Hawaii Research Center for Futures Studies (the "Manoa School"), argues that the future cannot be predicted and that futures studies really studies **images of the future**. After years of collecting them — from government plans, science fiction, polls, students — he concluded that nearly all of them fall into four generic piles:

1. **Continued Growth** — the official view of almost every modern institution: keep the economy growing and changing, with technology as the engine. By far the most common image.
2. **Collapse** — economic, environmental, social, or moral breakdown, from inside or outside, to a "lower" stage or to extinction.
3. **Discipline** — growth judged undesirable or unsustainable; society reorganises around survival, fair distribution and a set of fundamental values (natural, spiritual, religious, political or cultural).
4. **Transformation** — the transforming power of technology (AI, robotics, genetic engineering, space) remakes life, including humanity, into new, posthuman forms.

What makes this more than a list of scenarios is the set of rules Dator attaches to it:

- **No best case, worst case, or most likely case.** Each future has good and bad features. In the long run all four are equally probable, and all deserve equal sincerity.
- **Collapse is not the worst case.** Many people welcome it. Every disaster has winners as well as losers. All four futures are presented positively.
- **The four differ fundamentally** in their assumptions about how the world works. They are not high, medium and low versions of the same variables.
- **Never drop one.** More than four are allowed; fewer are not. Clients especially tend to skip Collapse.

The goal is to get people to *experience* a future different enough from the present to break the default assumption that the future is simply the present, extended. They then find how they could thrive there, and what to do now to move toward what they like in it and away from what they don't.

> **Naming note:** Inayatullah's retelling of the same model calls the third future *Steady state* (2008, p. 16), and he allows transformation to come through spiritual change as well as technology (p. 17). This skill uses Dator's names and definitions. When it uses Inayatullah's spiritual route, it says so.

> **fdbx adaptation:** we place the user's product or service inside each future and ask how it would thrive there. We add one *artifact from the future* per future, as Dator's workshop rooms do. And we end by listing the design decisions that hold in all four futures, which Dator's exercise does not ask for.

## Use This Skill When

- The roadmap, business case, or brief contains exactly one future, usually Continued Growth.
- The team treats one future as "the realistic one" and the rest as edge cases.
- You need to stress-test a product or service concept against very different worlds.
- You are preparing a futures workshop and need four future handouts.
- Before a preferred-future visioning exercise, so the vision is chosen rather than defaulted to.

## Inputs

- The subject: product, service, organisation, sector or place.
- A horizon year. Dator's default is roughly 20–50 years ahead; shorter horizons make the four futures harder to tell apart.
- Relevant history and present conditions. Dator's process starts with the past and the present before the futures (see `references/visioning-process.md`).
- Mode: **analysis** (the skill works through all four futures) or **workshop kit** (the skill produces four separate handouts for a group session).

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Ground in past and present

Briefly note how the subject came to be and its main problems and possibilities now. Record trends, emerging issues and continuities in the Evidence Ledger, each with its evidence type: `user-supplied`, `sourced`, or `assumption`. Whether something is a trend or an emerging issue belongs in the claim, not the Type column. Dator treats these as components 1–3 of the visioning process, and the four futures are built from their different mixes.

### Step 2 — Write the four futures

Write one future for each generic type, set in the horizon year. For each:

- Give it an evocative name, and state its generic type.
- State its underlying logic: what people in this world believe about how the world works. This is what makes the four *fundamentally* different.
- Write it **from inside and positively**, as a world people live in and make the best of, not as a warning.
- Create one **artifact from the future**: a news headline, a product notice, a diary entry, a public sign, a message from a service. (Dator's workshop rooms contain artifacts from each future.)

More than four futures are allowed, for example two varieties of Continued Growth, but every generic type must appear at least once.

### Step 3 — Experience each future: questions A–E

For each future, answer Dator's exercise questions:

- **A. Life in this future.** What are most people doing? Which of today's economic, environmental and other problems are gone or minor? What new problems do people worry about?
- **B. Probability.** How likely is this future? Discuss the evidence; do not rank the four against each other.
- **C. Preferability.** How close is it to what we would want?
- **D. Five things to do now** to move toward this future's desirable aspects.
- **E. Five things to do now** to prevent this future's undesirable aspects.

> **fdbx adaptation — the product in this future:** add a short paragraph on how the subject thrives in this world. What does it become, who uses it and why, and what does it stop doing?

### Step 4 — Across the four futures

> **fdbx adaptation.**

- **Robust design decisions:** choices that make sense in all four futures.
- **Single-future bets:** current decisions that only pay off in one future. Name which one.
- **Signals to watch:** for each future, one sign in the present that it is gaining ground. Record these in the Evidence Ledger.

### Step 5 — Toward a preferred future

Sketch, briefly, which aspects of which futures the team would want to combine. In Dator's process this is the start of the next component, envisioning a preferred future. It is a separate exercise, not a conclusion of this one.

### Workshop kit mode

Instead of Step 3's answers, produce:

- four **separate handouts**, each with one future's description and artifact, the "welcome to your future" instructions, and questions A–E
- a facilitation note: each group sees only its own future until the debrief; a group needs at least three people, so the exercise needs at least twelve; each group has a reporter, not a facilitator
- a debrief guide: read each future aloud before its group reports, then discuss how every group found a way to live in its future

See `references/workshop-guide.md`.

## Guardrails

- Never label a future best, worst, or most likely, in headings, prose, or ordering. Probability is discussed per future, not ranked.
- Present Collapse positively too: name who does well in it and how people live well there. Do not write it as a disaster movie.
- Do not treat Continued Growth as the baseline and the others as deviations. Give all four equal length and sincerity.
- Do not produce four versions of one variable (high/low adoption, fast/slow regulation). Each future must rest on a different belief about how the world works.
- Do not omit a future. Collapse is the one most often dropped.
- Use Dator's definitions. Transformation is technology-led in Dator; if you use Inayatullah's spiritual route, label it.
- In workshop kit mode, keep the four futures in separate handouts. Participants should not see the other futures before the debrief.
- Trends, emerging issues and signals go in the Evidence Ledger with their evidence type (`user-supplied`, `sourced`, or `assumption`).

## Deliverable Quality Bar

A strong Four Futures output:

- includes all four generic futures (Continued Growth, Collapse, Discipline, Transformation), each labelled with its type
- calls no future best, worst, or most likely, and describes Collapse with people who do well in it
- gives each future a distinct underlying logic rather than a different level of the same variable
- writes each future from inside, positively, with an artifact from that future
- answers A–E for each future, with five things to do now toward (D) and five against (E)
- places the subject in each future
- names at least three design decisions that hold in all four futures
- records trends and signals in the Evidence Ledger, each typed `user-supplied`, `sourced`, or `assumption`
- ends with a Handoff naming the next method and why
- opens with a direct answer to the decision or question the user asked, which the rest of the output supports
